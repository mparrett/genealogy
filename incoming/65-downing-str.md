The key finding: **65 Downing Street was the McCue family residence in January–May 1895**, and it was directly tied to a child-welfare intervention by the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. Catherine Lawless McCue’s death certificate gave the family address as 65 Downing Street, and the report says that in January 1895 the Society investigated “the McCue children who lived at 65 Downing Street.” Their father John McCue was hospitalized, Catherine was very ill, and the children were eventually sent through court to the Association for Befriending Children and Young Girls, run by the Sisters of Divine Compassion. 

That matters for your Higgins question because it shows **65 Downing was not just a random address**. At least in the 1890s, it was connected to an Irish/Catholic, working-class, distressed-family network where children were being evaluated for institutional placement. Your 1906 Protectory card naming **Mrs. Quigley at 65 Downing Street** fits that same kind of social geography: temporary caregivers, Catholic institutions, sick or absent parents, and court/charity involvement.

A few concrete details from the report:

Catherine McCue was living at **65 Downing Street** when the Society investigated on **21 Jan 1895**. The children were Thomas, John, Mary, and Margaret/Maggie. A family friend, **Mary Ward**, was caring for the three oldest children, while John McCue’s sister **Rebecca Leonard** was caring for Maggie. That gives you a model for what “Mrs. Quigley” may have been in James Higgins’s case: not necessarily a blood relative, but possibly a neighbor, friend, temporary caretaker, boarding-house woman, or Catholic-community contact. 

The McCue children were processed through court very quickly: Mary Ward brought the three older children to court the next day, and they were placed with the Association for Befriending Children and Young Girls. Later, Rebecca Leonard brought Maggie through a similar process. The report says these were handled through magistrate/court docket books, and that child-welfare cases could appear in criminal-style dockets under labels like vagrancy. That is a useful procedural clue for James Higgins: his 1906 Protectory commitment may also have left a court/docket trace outside the Protectory’s own intake file. 

The address also sits in a tight neighborhood cluster. The report places the McCues in **1890 at 351 Hudson Street**, and notes that a likely McCue relative, Thomas McCue, died at **357 Hudson Street**, less than a quarter-mile from 65 Downing Street. So the McCue family appears to have moved within a small Lower Manhattan/Greenwich Village working-class Irish Catholic zone rather than appearing at 65 Downing by accident.

**65 Downing Street may have been a multi-family tenement or boarding-house address with recurring Irish Catholic welfare/caregiver significance.** The report does not prove that Mrs. Quigley knew the McCues or the Higgins family, but it raises the odds that the building/address had a small local network of women who informally cared for children during crises.
