# The 1860 Consanguinity Dispensation — what it constrains

**Written:** 2026-08-20
**Record:** Croagh/Kilfinny RC marriage register, NLI `vtls000634972` **page 14**, verified against the image 2026-06-09
**Status:** analysis of an existing [T1] record. No new record was pulled to write this — this is reasoning from a fact already in the file that had not been worked through.

---

## The record

> **Novr. 18 [1860]** — *Patritius Gleeson de Croom & Maria Moloney de Kilfenny in Matrimonio conjuncti sunt, **habita dispensatione tam in Banniis quam in 3tio + 4to gradu Consanguinitatis**. Testes: Michaele Moloney & Joanne [Nash?]*

Two dispensations, not one: from the **banns**, and in the **third and fourth degree of consanguinity**.

---

## 1. What "3rd and 4th degree" actually means

The Church used the Germanic (canon) computation, not the Roman civil one. In that system:

| Degree | Relationship | Shared ancestor |
|---|---|---|
| 2nd | first cousins | grandparent |
| **3rd** | **second cousins** | **great-grandparent** |
| **4th** | **third cousins** | **great-great-grandparent** |

A **mixed** third-and-fourth degree means the two parties stood at **unequal remove** from the same ancestral couple: one was a great-grandchild, the other a great-great-grandchild.

**In modern terms Patrick and Margaret were second cousins once removed.**

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required a dispensation for any couple related within four degrees, so this was not exotic — but it was not free either, and it was recorded.

---

## 2. Dating the common ancestor — and a correction

Patrick was born c. 1833; Margaret on 31 December 1836. Working back at 25–35 years per generation:

| If… | Common ancestral couple born |
|---|---|
| Patrick is the **great-grandchild** (3 generations) | ~1728 – 1758 |
| Patrick is the **great-great-grandchild** (4 generations) | ~1693 – 1733 |

**Best estimate: the shared ancestral couple was born roughly 1710–1750, centring on 1725–1745.**

⚠️ **This corrects an earlier statement.** The figure "c. 1740–1770" was used in conversation on 2026-08-20 and is **too late by roughly a generation** — it was arrived at by loose arithmetic rather than by counting generations. The corrected window matters, because it puts the common ancestor **before** the start of any surviving register in either parish and well before the Tithe Applotment books. No baptism will ever name these people. They can only be reached by land records, wills, or the dispensation paperwork itself.

The mixed degree also tells us something in its own right: the two descent lines ran at **different speeds**. One reached 1833/1836 in three generations, the other in four. That is a real asymmetry, not a rounding artefact — one branch married young, or the other married late, consistently enough to fall a whole generation behind.

---

## 3. ⚠️ The shared ancestor need not be a Gleeson or a Moloney

**This corrects a second overstatement.** It has been said in this project — including by me — that the dispensation shows "the Gleeson and Moloney lines had already intermarried." **That does not follow.**

Patrick had eight great-grandparents and Margaret had sixteen great-great-grandparents. The common couple could sit on **any** of those lines. Patrick's known grandparental surnames include Gleeson and O'Brien; Margaret's include Molony and Kenny; the rest are unknown. The link could as easily be **O'Brien–Kenny**, or through two surnames we have never seen.

What the dispensation establishes is narrower and still valuable: **Patrick and Margaret descended from one common ancestral couple born c. 1710–1750.** The surname of that couple is unknown.

*(There is a soft reason to keep Gleeson–Moloney as the leading hypothesis — the two families are visibly interlaced in 1860s Croom: Michael Moloney witnesses this marriage, John Moloney sponsors Catherine's 1862 baptism, Ellen Moloney witnesses the 1864 O'Shea marriage, and Patrick stands godfather to a Moloney child in 1881. But that is **this** generation's association, and it is evidence about 1860, not about 1730. Hold it loosely.)*

---

## 4. Why this is the most powerful unexploited fact in the file

**It is a constraint that binds both pedigrees simultaneously.** Every other fact we hold pushes one line backwards in isolation. This one says the two lines are *required to meet* at a named generation. Build either side back far enough and it must produce the other. Nothing else in the project has that property.

That matters most because **the Gleeson side is name-saturated and getting worse.** The August 2026 sweep found at least four distinct James Gleeson couples in Hospital parish within a nine-year window, one of them living at Herbertstown itself. Ordinary record-matching there is close to useless. A structural constraint is worth more than another ambiguous name match.

**And the parish registers cannot answer it.** Croagh's registers begin in 1836 — the year Margaret was baptised. Hospital's are earlier but nowhere near 1730. The generations that would prove the link leave no sacramental trace at all.

---

## 5. ⭐ The migration insight — this may explain why Patrick moved

Patrick moved roughly **25 miles west**, from Hospital parish to Croom, between 1849 and 1859. That has been recorded as a bare fact with no explanation.

But he then married a woman from **Croagh/Kilfinny — adjacent to Croom — to whom he was already a blood relative.**

For a second-cousin-once-removed relationship to exist at all, **one of these must be true**:

- **(a)** one of Patrick's ancestral lines already reached into the Croom / Croagh / Kilfinny area, or
- **(b)** one of Margaret's already reached into Hospital / Emly.

Either way there was a **pre-existing kin corridor between the two districts**, running back at least to the mid-eighteenth century. Which reframes the migration entirely: Patrick very probably did **not** move into a void and happen to marry a distant cousin. He moved **toward known kin** — the standard Irish pattern — and married within the network that received him.

**Corroborating shape.** He was not the only one. **Stephen Gleeson**, sponsor at the 1829 Hospital baptism and our probable paternal uncle, *also* moved from the Hospital area to Croom (settling at Boherygeela), and *also* married with a consanguinity dispensation — **Mary Twomey, Croom, early March 1862, third degree**. Two Gleeson men out of the same Hospital family, both relocating to Croom, both marrying consanguineously within eighteen months of each other, is not two coincidences. It is a corridor.

---

## 6. What this predicts, and how to test it cheaply

The corridor hypothesis makes a **falsifiable prediction**: there should be Gleesons in the Croom / Croagh / Kilfinny area **before Patrick arrived**, or Molony/Kenny families in the Hospital / Emly area — or both.

| # | Test | Source | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Gleeson** householders in Croom, Croagh and Kilfinny civil parishes, ~1851 | Griffith's Valuation | free, online, searchable |
| 2 | **Molony** and **Kenny** householders in Hospital, Emly and Knockainy civil parishes, ~1851 | Griffith's Valuation | free, online, searchable |
| 3 | The same two, one generation earlier | Tithe Applotment Books, ~1825–35 | free, online |
| 4 | **Gleeson** at **Ballinvreena** and **Herbertstown**; **O'Brien** at Ballinvreena | Griffith's + Tithe | free |

**A positive result on test 1 or 2 converts the corridor from inference into geography** and gives the next generation of the search a named place to work in. A negative result on both is also informative: it would push the link onto the unknown surnames and make the dispensation paperwork the only remaining route.

Note the useful asymmetry: Griffith's for Co. Limerick is ~1851, which is **after** Patrick left Hospital but **before** he appears at Scagh in 1859. It therefore catches the corridor at exactly the right moment.

---

## 7. The dispensation from banns — a minor note, not over-read

They were dispensed from the **banns** as well. Reading intent into this would be over-reach: banns dispensations were routinely bought for convenience, to avoid a closed season, or simply to marry sooner. It is worth recording only because it means the marriage was **paid for twice over** — two dispensations — which is a small indicator that the families had money to spend on doing it properly. Patrick was a farmer at Scagh and later a shopkeeper; Margaret's people were Croom-area merchants. Consistent, no more.

---

## 8. Where the paperwork might be — and why it probably is not

A dispensation was granted on a **written petition to a bishop**, and the petition normally stated the relationship path, sometimes naming the common ancestor. That document is the one thing that would answer §2 and §3 outright.

Enquiries are drafted (not sent) to both plausible holders — see `research/outreach/gleeson-dispensation-inquiries-2026-08-20.md`:

- **Limerick Diocesan Archive** — the ceremony was at Croagh/Kilfinny, Diocese of Limerick. Bishop **John Ryan** (1828–1864).
- **Cashel & Emly, Thurles** — **and this may be the likelier holder**: dispensations were frequently granted by the bishop of the **groom's** diocese (the register formula *"ex episcopo sponsi"* records exactly that), and Patrick was a Hospital-parish man.

**Calibrate the expectation downward.** The Association for Church Archives of Ireland calls Limerick's nineteenth-century survival "disappointing", neither archive lists dispensations as a distinct series in its published holdings, and general guidance on Irish consanguinity dispensations confirms only that the *notation* survives in marriage registers — it is silent on whether petitions survive in diocesan custody. **Treat this as a long shot whose value lies in the asymmetry, not the probability.**
