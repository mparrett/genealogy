# Mary Knight (1866–1930)
**Relationship:** 2nd great-grandmother (Higgins line)

*Sligo Native, Irish Immigrant, Newark Wife and Mother*

Mary Knight was born in the mid-1860s in Ireland; family records and Ancestry profile data place her in County Sligo, on the rugged northwestern coast where the Atlantic meets the Ox Mountains. Sources give her birth as August 1866; an alternative tree puts it three years earlier, at 1863. Her parents were **James Knight** (b. 1843) and **Bridget Larney Knight** (1846–1894). The specific townland or parish has not been confirmed, and the Sligo origin itself is tentative — Mary Anne Higgins (Australia-branch researcher) notes that the surname **Larney** is not documented in Sligo per John Grenham's Irish Ancestors index, and a separate Theresa-line descendant records the variant surname **Courtney**. Whatever the truth of her townland, the family was among many post-Famine Irish households that left Ireland for opportunities in the American Northeast.

By 1880, when Mary was fourteen, the family had settled in Newark, New Jersey, where the U.S. census found her living among the city's growing Irish-American community. Newark in the 1880s was a place of immigrant labor—leather and shoe factories, breweries, the Pennsylvania Railroad—and a dense Catholic parish life. Mary's siblings included James J. (b. 1867), David H. (b. 1870), Mary Rebertha (1879–1902), and Elizabeth (b. 1879).

Three years later, in 1883, Mary married **Laurence Higgins** in Dublin on November 25. Laurence was then a 29-year-old Dublin laborer and widower with two surviving daughters from his first marriage to Mary Verner. Whether Mary had returned to Ireland for the wedding, or whether her family's American chapter had been temporary, the surviving record does not say.

By 1885 Mary was back in Newark, and on February 6, 1887, the couple married a second time at a ceremony in Manhattan—possibly to satisfy American legal recognition of their Irish marriage, or to formalize the union in an American parish. The witnesses to the 1887 ceremony were Christopher Higgins—Laurence's first cousin, eldest son of his paternal uncle Thomas Higgins, who had emigrated from Dublin to Jersey City in 1871—and Margaret McGrath.

Over the next dozen years Mary bore at least five children: Margaret (c. 1882), Mary A. (1888–1913), Lawrence (born and died in 1890 in infancy), **James Everett** (1892–1938), and Theresa Cecilia (1894–1980). Census records track the family through Newark's Ward 5 from 1895 through 1920, with a brief Jersey City interval around 1905. Laurence worked as a porter and later a janitor. Across these decades Mary appears in the census schedules as a wife and mother—without a recorded occupation. What she did with her days, whether she took in piecework or laundry, whether she organized her household alongside the rhythms of the parish, these textures of her life were not captured in the bureaucratic record.

The family endured a series of losses, and then a documented collapse. Mary's mother Bridget died in Newark in 1894. By about 1902 the two youngest children, James Everett and Theresa, had been placed at **St. Agatha's Home for Children** in Nanuet, New York—a Catholic orphanage in Rockland County, where they were enumerated together as inmates in the 1905 New York State Census. The crisis that broke the household is documented from an unexpected source: the reception card for James's August 1906 commitment to the New York Catholic Protectory records that his father Laurence had **absconded** from the family and that Mary herself was confined to the **insane asylum on Ward's Island**—the Manhattan State Hospital complex in the East River. James was then in the care of an aunt, Mrs. Quigley, at 65 Downing Street in Manhattan.

That Mary was at Ward's Island in 1906 is the first documented evidence of her institutionalization. The period's institutional language was broad and the card's single line cannot reconstruct her diagnosis—it could mark psychiatric illness, exhaustion, chronic disease, or simply destitution-driven confinement. What is certain is that she was discharged. By the 1910 federal census she was again with Laurence in Newark, where they appear together a second time in 1920. The children's paths diverged: James was committed to the Protectory and was gone west within fifteen years, while Theresa remained at Clarkstown for at least another decade, enumerated there in 1910 and again in the 1915 New York State Census at age twenty as a "servant"—evidently kept on as institutional staff after aging out of the inmate roll. Eight years after Mary's Ward's Island episode, in 1913, daughter **Mary A. Higgins Hamm**—born in Jersey City in 1888, married into the Hamm family—died at St. Joseph's Hospital in the Bronx at age twenty-five.

Laurence Higgins died sometime before February 17, 1930. We know this because that is the date Mary was admitted to a New York City almshouse as a widow. The exact date and place of Laurence's death have not been confirmed. Six weeks later, on March 27, 1930, Mary herself died at the **NYC Cancer Institute** in Manhattan—having been transferred from the almshouse, the records do not say whether for treatment or palliative care. She was buried at Holy Name Cemetery and Mausoleum in Jersey City, New Jersey—Plot I I 7-8—beside her infant son Lawrence, reunited in the ground after forty years.

Why a sixty-three-year-old widow with surviving adult children was admitted to an almshouse just weeks after her husband's death—whether through eviction, ill health, or long-standing destitution—the present record does not say. For Mary, it was the second documented passage through a public institution, twenty-four years after Ward's Island. Mary Knight's story arrives in fragments: a Sligo birth, an Atlantic crossing, a Dublin wedding, a Manhattan second ceremony, decades of motherhood in Irish-American Newark, a Ward's Island confinement and return, a final passage through cancer ward and almshouse alike. Her grandson Howard Robert Higgins would carry the family forward to Arizona, where the Higgins line continues today.

**Open questions:**

- What townland or parish in County Sligo was Mary born in?
- When did the Knight family emigrate, and through what port? When did James Knight die?
- Whether Mary returned to Ireland in 1883 for the wedding, or had been there for some interval before.
- What occupation, if any, Mary held beyond keeping house.
- When Mary was admitted to Ward's Island and when she was discharged. (We have her there as of Aug 1906 and back with Laurence by the 1910 census; the admission/discharge dates and her recorded condition remain unknown.)
- Whether her 1930 admission to the NYC almshouse reflects a recurrence of the 1906 institutional pattern or a separate, terminal event tied to the cancer that killed her six weeks later.
- The identity of "Mrs. Quigley, 65 Downing Street"—working hypothesis is a paternal aunt by marriage (a Higgins sibling who married a Quigley); Knight side not ruled out.
- The exact date and circumstances of Laurence's death, and the chain of events that brought Mary to the almshouse so quickly afterward.
