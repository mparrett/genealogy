James Everett Higgins (1892–1938)

Relationship: Great-grandfather (Higgins line)

Orphan, Railroad Engineer, WWI Veteran

James Everett Higgins was born on February 2, 1892, at 12 Clarkson Street in Manhattan, New York—the fourth of five children born to Laurence Higgins (1854–1930) and Mary Knight (1866–1930). His parents were Irish immigrants who lived in the Newark and Jersey City area, where Laurence worked as a porter. James's early years were shaped by the tight-knit but struggling Irish-American community of urban New Jersey.

By 1905, when James was thirteen, he and his younger sister Theresa had been living for several years at St. Agatha's Home for Children in Nanuet, New York—a Catholic orphanage in Rockland County. The 1905 New York State Census enumerated them both as inmates in Clarkstown, Enumeration District 05. The placement was no temporary stopgap: the New York Catholic Protectory's later reception card recorded that James had been at St. Agatha's for four years, placing his arrival around 1902, when he was about nine.

What had driven the family to that pass is now documented. By the time James was committed to the Protectory in 1906, his father Laurence had absconded from the household, and his mother Mary was confined to the insane asylum on Ward's Island—the Manhattan State Hospital complex in the East River. James himself was living at 65 Downing Street, a few blocks from the Clarkson Street and Hudson Street addresses his parents had kept in the 1890s, in the care of an aunt the card names only as "Mrs. Quigley." Whether she was kin on the Higgins side or the Knight side has not yet been established.

In the summer of 1906 James was placed at St. Vincent's Hospital in Greenwich Village—some kind of minor work or apprenticeship arrangement beginning July 6. Within weeks the hospital described him as "unmanageable" and requested his formal commitment. On August 2, 1906 he was received at the New York Catholic Protectory in the Bronx as Reception No. 40130, State Board No. 19739. The card notes he was confirmed in the Catholic faith and could read and write. He was committed for destitution by Hon. R. C. Baker, First Deputy Commissioner of Charities, and physically brought in by Mr. O'Shea of the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. He was carried "free" on the Protectory's books for three weeks while the city worked out who would pay; on August 22 he was transferred to the regular City Bill, the public welfare apparatus taking responsibility for his upkeep.

He remained at the Protectory through his teenage years. The 1910 federal census, enumerated on May 9, found him still in the Bronx as a Protectory resident, age 17. Two weeks earlier, on April 27, 1910, he had been sent "on trial"—institutional shorthand for a transitional aftercare placement—to St. Philip's Home, 417 Broome Street, a halfway house for working boys leaving Catholic institutional care. He was employed by Peter Duryea at 69 Pearl Street in lower Manhattan. By June 25 he had moved out of St. Philip's and into a boarding arrangement at 1192 First Avenue, care of a Kiernan. Sometime in the years that followed he made his way west to the Pacific Northwest.

When the United States entered World War I, James enlisted in July 1918, listing his residence as the Langhan Hotel in Spokane, Washington. He served as a wagoner—driving supply wagons and handling horses—and remained stateside until his discharge on June 9, 1919.

By 1921 James had settled in California and was working as a civil engineer with the Western Pacific Railroad, helping run the line through Feather River Canyon—the last major railroad route carved through the Sierra Nevada, an engineering marvel of tunnels and trestles hugging granite cliffs above the river. A newspaper that year noted that "James E. Higgins is an earnest, hard-working young civil engineer, whose friends believe he will go far."

In 1924, James's sister Theresa placed a newspaper advertisement searching for him: "James Higgins, 32, Formerly of N.Y., Sought by Sister." The clipping noted that their mother, Mary, wished to see her son. The siblings had lost touch during the war years and James's constant movement working the western railroads. Whether the appeal ever reached him is not known.

James married Doris Pauline Kuthe on November 6, 1926, in Phoenix, Arizona. Doris was the daughter of George Washington Kuthe, an Oregon farmer who had relocated to Arizona, and granddaughter of Oregon Trail pioneers. The marriage connected the Higgins line—rooted in Dublin and Newark—to the Kuthe family's westward journey from Missouri to Oregon to Arizona.

Their son Howard Robert Higgins was born on September 15, 1927, in Tucson. But the marriage was short-lived. Doris, an ambitious young teacher building a career in Arizona's early education system, filed for divorce in December 1928. The divorce was finalized by 1930, with Doris living in Buckeye and working as a teacher. She remained close with the Birch family, friends who would later play an important role in young Howard's life. That same spring, far across the country, James's mother Mary died on March 27, 1930, at the NYC Cancer Institute in Manhattan, six weeks after being admitted to a New York City almshouse as a widow. She was buried at Holy Name Cemetery in Jersey City, beside her infant son Lawrence. Theresa's 1924 plea on her behalf, so far as the record shows, went unanswered.

James returned to California, continuing his railroad work and trying to make ends meet through the lean years of the Depression. By 1932, legal judgments had been filed against him—signs of the financial troubles that plagued many during that era. He lived at various San Francisco addresses, including 516 O'Farrell Street. In his final years, James found work with the WPA on one of the era's most ambitious engineering projects: the construction of Treasure Island in San Francisco Bay, a man-made island built for the 1939 Golden Gate International Exposition.

On July 6, 1938, James Everett Higgins died in San Francisco at age forty-six. He was found in a "lodger's cell" at the city jail—not incarcerated, but using the free overnight shelter the jail provided for men who had nowhere else to go. Newspapers reported he had recently paid off his debts before taking his own life. As a veteran, he was buried two days later with military honors.

James Higgins's life traced an arc familiar to many of his generation: from immigrant roots and childhood hardship, through self-made success in the booming industries of the American West, to the crushing weight of the Great Depression. Though he did not live to see his son grow up, Howard would carry forward the connection to Arizona that James's brief marriage to Doris had established—though raised by the Birch family, with the Higgins name a quiet part of his heritage discovered only by later generations. Howard married Patricia Anne Mowrey, a descendant of the Gleeson and Mowery lines, weaving together family threads that continue today.

James's legacy as an engineer echoed through the generations. Howard became an electrical engineer; his son Mike combined talents in music and computing; and later descendants would carry the technical tradition forward into software engineering—a through-line from the railroad surveys of the 1910s to the digital age.
