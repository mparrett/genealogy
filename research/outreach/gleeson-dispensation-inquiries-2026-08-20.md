## Why these were written

Patrick Gleeson and Margaret Moloney married at Croagh/Kilfinny on **18 November 1860** *"habita dispensatione tam in Banniis quam in **3tio + 4to gradu Consanguinitatis**"* — dispensed from the banns **and** in the third and fourth degree of consanguinity.

**What the degrees mean.** In the standard Irish Catholic reckoning: 2nd degree = first cousins (shared grandparent); 3rd = second cousins (shared great-grandparent); 4th = third cousins (shared great-great-grandparent). A **mixed 3rd-and-4th** means the two parties stood at *unequal* remove from the common ancestor — one a great-grandchild, the other a great-great-grandchild. In modern terms Patrick and Margaret were roughly **second cousins once removed**, and the shared ancestral couple was born somewhere around **1740–1770**.

**Why it is the highest-value unexploited fact in the file.** It is a *structural* constraint that binds both lines at once: any Moloney pedigree built backwards constrains the Gleeson pedigree, and vice versa, because the two are required to meet at a named generation. Nothing else in this project has that property. And the Gleeson side badly needs the help — Hospital parish held **at least four distinct James Gleeson couples** in a nine-year window, so ordinary record-matching there is close to useless.

Crucially, the parish registers **cannot** answer it: Croagh's registers begin only in 1836, so the generations that would prove the link leave no trace. A surviving dispensation petition would state the relationship path and sometimes name the common ancestor outright.

**A second dispensation exists in the same family:** **Stephen Gleeson × Mary Twomey**, Croom, early March 1862, **third degree**. Stephen is our probable paternal uncle on the strength of having sponsored the 1829 Hospital baptism. Two petitions from one extended family, eighteen months apart.

**Why Cashel & Emly as well — this is not redundancy.** A dispensation was often obtained from **the bishop of the *groom's* diocese**, not the bride's (the standard register formula *"ex episcopo sponsi"* records exactly this). Patrick was a Hospital-parish man, so **Cashel & Emly may in fact be the more likely holder of the 1860 petition than Limerick.** Both should be asked.

The draft also seeds the family identifiers — the 17 March 1833 Emly baptism to James Gleeson and Honora O'Brien at Ballinvriana, the move to Herbertstown by 1838, and Stephen Gleeson as an 1829 Hospital sponsor.

## ⚠️ Calibration — this is a long shot, and the drafts say so

Both letters are written to make a **negative answer easy and useful**, because a negative is the likelier outcome:

- The Association for Church Archives of Ireland describes Limerick's holdings as "a small but important collection" and explicitly calls nineteenth-century survival **"disappointing."**
- Neither archive's published holdings summary mentions dispensations or pre-nuptial enquiries as a distinct series.
- General guidance on Irish consanguinity dispensations confirms the *notation* survives in marriage registers but is silent on whether *petitions* survive in diocesan custody.

So: **low probability, very low cost, and an enormous payoff if it lands.** That asymmetry is the whole argument for sending. A clean "we hold nothing of that kind" is worth having, because it closes the route permanently instead of leaving it as a standing maybe.

Cashel & Emly's papers are stated as accessible **up to the death of Archbishop Croke in 1903**, so the 1860s fall inside their open period.

## If both come back empty

Fall back to the cheaper Margaret-side work, which does not depend on anyone's goodwill:

1. **Sweep Croagh/Kilfinny baptisms 1836–1855** for children of **James Molony × Catherine Kenny** — the same technique that took the Gleeson sibling set from four to nine. Margaret was baptised 31 Dec 1836 and the register begins that year, so older siblings are invisible, but younger ones are findable and each brings **sponsors**, which is how the kin network gets mapped. This is also the only way to test whether **James Moloney, the 1881 Croom shopkeeper**, is really her brother — currently pure naming-rule inference.
2. **Tithe Applotment for Ballinvreena (~1826–34)** — tests whether the Gleesons were on O'Brien ground in 1833.
3. **Griffith's and the Valuation Office revision books** for Scagh, Herbertstown and Ballinvreena.
