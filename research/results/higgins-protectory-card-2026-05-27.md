# James Higgins — New York Catholic Protectory / Lincolndale Resident Identification Card Research Note

## Working conclusion

This Resident Identification Card is a high-confidence match for James Everett Higgins.

The identification is strong because the card aligns on the main identity anchors: James Higgins; father Lawrence/Laurence, born Ireland; mother Mary, born Ireland; child born in New York; Catholic family; prior residence with an aunt; and prior placement at St. Agatha’s Home, Nanuet. The card also gives the duration at St. Agatha’s as four years, which helps explain why James appears in the Nanuet/St. Agatha’s orbit before the Protectory record.

The identification is now reinforced by the 1905 New York State Census: James Higgins, age 13, born about 1892 in the United States, appears as an inmate in Clarkstown, Rockland County, New York, enumeration district 05. His sister Theresa also appears in the same institutional context. This places both children in the Nanuet/Clarkstown institutional setting before James’s 1906 Protectory commitment.

James’s civil birth record gives a formal birth date of February 2, 1892 in New York, New York, certificate no. 6077. The Protectory card gives August 2, 1892, which may reflect an institutional recording error, a copied date from his commitment/reception date, or a mistaken family/institutional statement. The safest current statement is: James was officially recorded at birth as born February 2, 1892, while the Protectory card later recorded August 2, 1892; both agree on the key year, 1892.

Depending on whether the “four years” was counted back from the 1906 reception or approximated from memory, James likely entered St. Agatha’s around 1901–1902, when he was about nine or ten.

This is unusually concrete. It gives a pre-commitment work lead and suggests he was already being managed through institutional or charitable channels shortly before his formal Protectory commitment.

This likely marks transition from institutional housing to a boarding arrangement while working.

## Entity guide and interpretation

### New York Catholic Protectory

The Protectory was a Catholic child-welfare institution for destitute, truant, neglected, or otherwise dependent Catholic children. James’s card fits the Protectory’s routine administrative framework: reception number, reason for commitment, parents’ status, religion, residence, public/private payment status, and later aftercare notes.

### St. Agatha’s Home, Nanuet

The card states James had been an inmate at St. Agatha’s Home for four years. The 1905 New York State Census independently fits this placement, showing James as an inmate in Clarkstown, Rockland County. Theresa’s presence in the same institutional context turns this from an isolated James record into a sibling-family institutional placement.

Research target: St. Agatha’s admission/discharge records, Catholic Charities / Archdiocese records, annual reports, and any county or city payment records for James and Theresa.

### Clarkson Street / Hudson Street / Downing Street cluster

The connected addresses create a tight lower-Manhattan family geography:

- Clarkson Street: Lawrence Higgins’s residence when James was born on February 2, 1892.
- 409 Hudson Street: Laurence/Lawrence Higgins’s residence when Theresa Cecilia Higgins was born on July 30, 1894.
- 65 Downing Street: James’s residence at time of 1906 commitment, with aunt Mrs. Quigley.

These addresses sit in the same small west-side neighborhood cluster. That matters because it makes Mrs. Quigley less likely to be a random institutional caretaker and more likely to be real kin or a close family-network caretaker already known to the Higgins family.

### 65 Downing Street, Mrs. Quigley

This is now a major family-network lead.

The current working hypothesis is that Lawrence/Laurence Higgins had a sibling who married a Quigley. That would make Mrs. Quigley James’s paternal aunt by marriage or, if the sibling was female, his paternal aunt under her married name. Mary’s side should not be ruled out yet, but the emerging evidence appears to point toward the Higgins side.

Research targets: 1905 NY State Census, 1900 U.S. Census, 1910 U.S. Census, city directories, and Catholic parish registers near Downing Street. Search for Quigley households at or near 65 Downing Street, then map adult women in those households back to possible Irish-born Higgins/Knight families. Also search for Higgins–Quigley and Knight–Quigley marriages in New York City and nearby counties.

### Mr. O’Shea, S.P.C.C.

S.P.C.C. almost certainly refers to the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. Mr. O’Shea was likely an officer, agent, or investigator who physically brought James to the Protectory or handled the referral.

Research targets: NYSPCC annual reports around 1905–1907, staff/officer lists, case records, and any case file involving James Higgins, Lawrence Higgins, Mary Higgins, St. Vincent’s Hospital, or 65 Downing Street.

### Department of Charities / Hon. R. C. Baker

The Department of Charities was the New York City public-welfare authority involved in poor relief, institutional care, and public payment for dependent persons. R. C. Baker appears on the card as the 1st Deputy Commissioner of Charities and the formal committing authority.

This helps explain the “Free List,” “regular City Bill,” and “Public Pay Roll” language. James’s status was partly administrative and financial: who had legal or public responsibility for paying the Protectory for his care.

Research targets: NYC Municipal Archives / DORIS Department of Public Charities records, annual reports, commissioner correspondence, commitment ledgers, and payment rolls.

### Ward’s Island / mother Mary

The card says Mary, James’s mother, was “in the insane asylum, Ward’s Island” or “on Ward’s Island.” In 1906 this points toward Manhattan State Hospital / the Ward’s Island asylum complex.

The wording should not be treated as a precise diagnosis. It is an institutional label from the period. It could reflect psychiatric illness, chronic illness, incapacity, poverty-related confinement, or legal/institutional classification rather than a modern medical diagnosis.

Research targets: Manhattan State Hospital patient indexes and admission/discharge records for Mary Higgins, especially around 1902–1906. Use variants: Mary Higgins, Mary Knight Higgins, Maria Higgins, Marie Higgins, May Higgins, and possible age/birthplace Ireland.

### St. Vincent’s Hospital

The note says James was employed at St. Vincent’s Hospital beginning July 6, 1906 and had become “unmanageable.” This probably means he was placed in some kind of minor work, messenger, helper, orderly, errand, kitchen, laundry, or institutional labor role. It does not necessarily mean formal employment in the modern sense.

Research targets: St. Vincent’s Hospital records, Sisters of Charity records, annual reports, staff/payroll/minor employee records if extant, and newspaper references to boys employed at the hospital.

### “Unmanageable”

This is administrative language, not a personality verdict. In context it probably means the hospital or supervising agency could no longer manage him in a work-placement setting and requested formal commitment.

This detail helps explain why a child previously at St. Agatha’s and briefly employed at St. Vincent’s ended up at the Protectory: the system was trying placements, but family support was absent or unstable.

### “Sent on trial”

This almost certainly means a trial placement, not a criminal trial.

In child-welfare and institutional language of the period, “on trial” often meant a child or young person was placed out temporarily to test whether a home, work, apprenticeship, or boarding arrangement would succeed. The Protectory tracked outcomes after trial placements.

### St. Philip’s Home, 417 Broome Street

St. Philip’s Home was connected to the Protectory ecosystem and functioned as transitional housing / aftercare for older boys, especially working boys leaving institutional care.

This fits James’s age and timeline very well. By April 1910 he was about 18, so St. Philip’s was a plausible stepping stone between the Protectory and independent boarding/employment.

### Peter Duryea, 69 Pearl Street

This is a concrete employment lead.

The note does not say James lived with Peter Duryea; it says he was employed by him. 69 Pearl Street was likely a business address in lower Manhattan. Research should focus on city directories, commercial directories, tax records, and 1910 Manhattan business listings for Peter Duryea / Duryea at 69 Pearl Street.

### 1192 First Avenue, care of Kiernan

This likely indicates a boarding address after James left St. Philip’s Home or after a trial period. “c/o Kiernan” likely names the boarding-house keeper, landlord, employer contact, or supervising adult.

Research targets: 1910 U.S. Census enumeration for 1192 First Avenue, Manhattan; city directories for Kiernan at that address; building directories; and neighborhood parish records.

## Working narrative paragraph

James Everett Higgins was born in Manhattan on February 2, 1892, the son of Lawrence/Laurence Higgins, an Irish-born laborer, and Mary Higgins. The family’s early records cluster tightly around the lower west side of Manhattan: Clarkson Street at James’s birth, 409 Hudson Street at Theresa’s birth in 1894, and 65 Downing Street by James’s 1906 commitment. By 1905, James and Theresa were in the St. Agatha’s/Nanuet institutional world in Clarkstown, Rockland County. The Protectory card later states that James had been at St. Agatha’s for four years. By 1906, family support had collapsed: his father had absconded; his mother was institutionalized on Ward’s Island; and James was living or legally associated with his aunt, Mrs. Quigley, at 65 Downing Street. In July 1906 he was placed or employed at St. Vincent’s Hospital, but within weeks he was described as unmanageable and was committed for destitution to the New York Catholic Protectory. The commitment was handled through the Department of Charities and physically brought through the S.P.C.C. agent Mr. O’Shea. By 1908 his public-payment status changed, and in 1910 he was moved into the Protectory’s older-boy aftercare/work system: first sent on trial to St. Philip’s Home at 417 Broome Street, employed by Peter Duryea at 69 Pearl Street, and then boarding at 1192 First Avenue in care of Kiernan. This card therefore connects James’s birth family, neighborhood cluster, sibling institutionalization, family breakdown, public-welfare status, early work placement, and transition into independent working life.
